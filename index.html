Anchoring the Chromospheric Activity$-$Rotation Relation: A Study of the Pleiades and Group X
Authors
A. Núñez, M. A. Agüeros, J. L. Curtis, C. A. Ospinal Achicanoy
Comments
Accepted for publication in the AAS Journals
Primary
astro-ph.SR
Categories
astro-ph.SR astro-ph.GA
We present a study of the rotation and the chromospheric H$α$ activity of low-mass stars in the $\approx$120 Myr-old Pleiades and the $\approx$400 Myr-old Group X open clusters. We combine published and new rotation periods ($P_\mathrm{rot}$) from TESS and ZTF, and use LAMOST, SDSS, and MDM spectra to measure H$α$ equivalent widths (EWs). While overall the Group X distributions of $P_\mathrm{rot}$ and EWs are as expected, detailed comparisons to the Pleiades and to the 700-Myr-old benchmark Praesepe produce some surprises. For F through mid K stars, the Group X color$-$period sequence lies between those of the Pleiades and of Praesepe, as expected. But the $P_\mathrm{rot}$ for its late K and early M dwarfs and the Praesepe slow-rotator sequence overlap, indicating that the 700 Myr-old stars have not spun down significantly despite being nearly twice as old as their Group X counterparts. In the Rossby number$-L_\mathrm{Hα}/L_\mathrm{bol}$ plane, rapid rotators in Group X are saturated, as is seen in older clusters, whereas the rapid rotators in the Pleiades show a weak but measurable dependence of $L_\mathrm{Hα}/L_\mathrm{bol}$ on rotation. This implies that the chromospheric activity$-$rotation relation attains its mature form between 120 and 400 Myr. Dividing the sample by color reveals that K, M0, and M1 dwarfs are no longer saturated by $\approx$120 Myr, M2 and M3 dwarfs become unsaturated between $\approx$400 and $\approx$700 Myr, and later M dwarfs remain largely saturated through field ages even as their saturated $L_\mathrm{Hα}/L_\mathrm{bol}$ levels decline, indicating that chromospheric evolution is not determined by rotation period alone.

Young massive star clusters (YMCs; ${\gtrsim}10^4\rm\,M_\odot$) represent a distinct, extreme mode of star formation and the closest present-day analogues of the environments in which globular clusters and Cosmic Noon stellar populations assembled. Given the extreme dust extinction in the earliest phase of YMC formation, (sub-)millimeter observations are the only means of probing their assembly and early feedback. Pioneering works with ALMA are limited to individual region studies and to the most massive YMCs in them, leaving many open questions about the initial conditions of YMC formation, the integrated star and cluster formation efficiencies, the dominant pre-supernova feedback channels, and the prevalence and reach of cluster-driven outflows. ALMA2040 will enable mass-complete, resolved surveys in hundreds of host galaxies, transforming the study of massive cluster formation and feedback from individual case analyses into statistical measurements across the full range of galactic environments.

We used a combination of James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) and Hubble Space Telescope (HST) observations to investigate the mass functions (MFs) of the different stellar populations in the globular cluster (GC) 47 Tucanae (NGC 104) over a wide stellar-mass interval. We identified the multiple populations along the lower main sequence using only JWST photometry and, in particular, colour combinations sensitive to variations in oxygen abundance. We derived the MFs of the first- and second-population (1P and 2P) stars separately and found that in the low-mass regime, the present-day MF of the 1P population is significantly steeper than that of the 2P population, which appears nearly flat. We also present the results of a dynamical model showing that this behaviour requires a different initial mass function (IMF) for the 2P and the 1P populations in the 0.1-0.4 solar masses mass range, thus providing a new fundamental constraint for the study of the star formation of multiple populations in GCs.

The discovery of massive quiescent galaxies at high redshift by JWST has revealed that star formation can cease rapidly in the early Universe, yet the physical mechanisms responsible for quenching remain unknown. A key missing element is the systematic measurement in statistically representative quiescent samples of the cold gas and dust content, which are crucial for distinguishing quenching mechanisms and evolutionary pathways. Such observations are currently not feasible because state-of-the-art sub-millimeter facilities (e.g., ALMA) cannot routinely detect the faint molecular gas and dust reservoirs of quiescent galaxies, nor pinpoint outflowing molecular gas. A next-generation facility (ALMA2040) capable of providing transformative access to the cold interstellar medium of quiescent galaxies across redshift is therefore essential. In particular, it will allow the community to probe the molecular gas content of quiescent galaxies at cosmic noon and beyond by targeting the CO, [CI], and [CII] emission lines, as well as unveiling the physical conditions of the cold interstellar medium by constraining the CO spectral line energy distribution. It will also reveal the dust content of the passive population, a crucial ingredient to constrain the different quenching mechanisms. Finally, it will enable probing the molecular gas kinematics and identify molecular outflows driven by supermassive black holes. When combined with the rich legacy of JWST, ELT, and SKAO surveys, ALMA2040 will deliver a comprehensive, multi-phase view of feedback and quenching, allowing us to establish when, how, and why galaxies shut down their star formation across cosmic time.

Diffuse X-ray emission in galaxies originates from stellar-feedback-heated gas and unresolved compact sources. We investigate its thermal component and the scaling of X-ray luminosity with star formation rate (SFR), including galaxies with very low SFRs. We analyzed eROSITA/SRG data from the four all-sky surveys (eRASS:4) for a blind, volume-limited sample of 140 galaxies within 25 Mpc from the Updated Nearby Galaxy Catalog (Karachentsev et al. 2013), cross-matched with HECATEv2 (Kyritsis et al. 2026b). Early-type galaxies were analyzed separately, while star-forming galaxies were divided into low-, medium-, and high-specific-SFR (sSFR) subsamples. We modeled the integrated X-ray spectra with thermal plasma and power-law components, stacked spectra for each subsample, and derived total and thermal X-ray luminosities. The stacked spectra show a stronger soft thermal component at high sSFR, whereas low-sSFR and early-type galaxies are increasingly dominated by the harder non-thermal continuum. For star-forming galaxies, the total 0.5-8 keV luminosity follows $\log L_X \sim 39.5 + 0.9\log\mathrm{SFR}$, with an intrinsic scatter of about 0.9 dex. The 0.5-2 keV thermal luminosity follows $\log L_X \sim 38.7 + 1.1\log\mathrm{SFR}$, with an intrinsic scatter of about 0.7 dex. The thermal luminosity remains well correlated with SFR down to $\sim 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{M}_\odot\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ and is consistent with previous relations for star-forming galaxies. By contrast, the flatter and more scattered total X-ray luminosity-SFR relation suggests that unresolved X-ray binaries likely dominate at low SFR, while thermal X-ray emission remains a robust tracer of stellar feedback.

We combine wide-area COSMOS-3D observations with deeper GOODS-N data from JWST/NIRCam F444W wide-field slitless spectroscopy to trace cosmic star formation over $z\sim0$--9. The observations cover approximately $0.33~\mathrm{deg}^2$ in COSMOS and $60.9~\mathrm{arcmin}^2$ in GOODS-N, with typical $3σ$ integrated line-flux limits of $3.2\times10^{-18}$ and $1.04\times10^{-18}~\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}\,cm^{-2}}$, respectively. The common $3.9$--$5.0\,μ$m wavelength window probes Br$α$, Br$β$, Pa$α$, Pa$β$, H$α$, and H$β$ across the local Universe, cosmic noon, and reionization. Our final sample contains 9146 galaxies, including 7883 in COSMOS and 1263 in GOODS-N. COSMOS constrains luminous emitters, while GOODS-N extends the luminosity functions toward fainter sources. We measure dust-corrected line luminosities, account for line-detection completeness, and convert the six lines to equivalent H$α$ luminosities assuming Case B recombination. Schechter fits reveal an increase in the characteristic H$α$-equivalent luminosity toward cosmic noon. Integrating the resulting star formation rate functions above $0.24\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$, we trace the cosmic star formation rate density from the local Universe to reionization. The combined Brackett, Paschen, and Balmer measurements are broadly consistent with the canonical UV- and IR-based cosmic star formation history at $z\lesssim6$. Fixed-slope tests show that the H$β$ estimate at $z\sim8$ remains sensitive to the external photometric faint-end constraint and extrapolation below our spectroscopic limit. These measurements provide complementary constraints on the distribution and integrated rate of star formation across cosmic time.

JWST has revealed a population of "dormant'' galaxies at z>3 with strong Balmer breaks and weak emission lines, whose star formation appears to have declined only recently. Whether these galaxies are undergoing a lasting transition toward quiescence or merely experiencing a temporary suppression of star formation remains unclear. We investigate their molecular gas content using archival ALMA observations of [CII]158um and CO rotational transitions. Starting from all publicly available JWST/NIRSpec prism observations and using joint spectrophotometric fitting, we construct a parent sample at z>3 and log(Mstar/Msun)>9.5 in the ALMA-accessible COSMOS, UDS, and GOODS-South fields. Our final sample comprises 7 dormant galaxies and 15 quiescent galaxies with ALMA constraints on their molecular gas content (mostly 3 sigma upper limits). We find that dormant galaxies exhibit systematically lower star formation rates averaged over the past 10Myr (SFR10Myr) than over the past 100Myr (SFR100Myr), supporting a recent rapid decline in their star formation. For the ~70% of the sample with sufficiently constraining ALMA observations, their molecular gas fractions lie at least ~0.3-2dex below those expected for main-sequence galaxies. Moreover, the molecular gas fractions of dormant galaxies show no systematic difference from those of quiescent galaxies in our sample. This indicates that dormant galaxies have already reached a gas-poor state shortly after quenching and favors a scenario in which the rapid shutdown of star formation is associated with depletion or removal of the molecular gas reservoir, rather than primarily with suppressed star formation efficiency while retaining a substantial molecular gas reservoir. Our results suggest that, if no substantial gas replenishment occurs in the future, dormant galaxies are more consistent with becoming "dead'' than with being temporarily "sleeping''.

In galaxies, stellar populations represent a superposition of both in situ and accreted components, tracing internal chemical evolution as well as the histories of merged satellites. We investigate how assembly history shapes the chemical properties of present-day stellar populations in low-mass dwarf galaxies. We analyze a suite of EDGE-INFERNO star-by-star cosmological zoom-in simulations of galaxies with present-day halo mass $\sim1.4\times10^9\,{\rm M}_{\odot}$. We systematically vary galaxy growth while preserving the present-day halo mass, engineering our simulations to span a continuum of assembly histories. Early-forming systems form most of their stars in a single progenitor, with centrally concentrated star formation and a continuous enrichment history. In contrast, our late-forming systems assemble by accretion of multiple progenitors with their own stellar populations, resulting in fragmented star formation across several halos with independent chemical evolution tracks. These regimes produce qualitatively different chemical signatures: single-progenitor systems exhibit connected, well-defined trends in abundance space, while multi-progenitor systems show multiple, distinct stellar populations with limited overlap. We find that metallicity distribution functions provide initial constraints on assembly history for samples of at least $50$-$100$ member stars. Multi-element abundance analysis significantly improves the recovery of distinct components, although robust reconstruction still requires larger samples. Fully separating in situ and accreted populations likely requires kinematic information. Accreted stars can contribute substantially even at high metallicities in multi-progenitor systems, while external enrichment can cause accreted material to follow similar chemical trends as in situ stars, particularly in the single-progenitor regime.

Large-scale stellar counter-rotation, in which two coplanar stellar components rotate in opposite directions, is usually interpreted as a signature of external gas accretion. Such systems are most often found in relatively sparse environments, where cold gas can be acquired and remain dynamically undisturbed long enough to settle into a disk. It is less clear whether this channel can operate in massive central galaxies, whose group or cluster environments can heat, strip, or perturb cold gas. Here we present NGC 5717, one of the most massive galaxies known to host a counter-rotating stellar disk and the first identified case in a brightest group galaxy. Using integral-field spectroscopy from SDSS-IV Mapping Nearby Galaxies at Apache Point Observatory (MaNGA), together with CFHT imaging, ancillary HI data, and SDSS/DESI spectroscopy of the surrounding group, we show that the galaxy contains a dynamically cold counter-rotating disk embedded in an old dynamically hot elliptical component. The disk is young ($\sim 1.3$ Gyr), metal-poor ([Fe/H] $\sim -0.7$ dex), extended ($h \sim 2.4$ kpc), and massive ($\log M_\star/M_\odot \sim 9.9$); the associated ionized gas co-rotates with it, while the available HI data indicate no significant remaining reservoir of neutral atomic gas ($\log M_{\mathrm{HI}}/M_\odot \lesssim 9$). These properties support an in situ origin of the secondary component from externally accreted gas. The chemical evolution model favors gas accretion from a cosmic filament, which is consistent with the surrounding cosmic-web geometry, and indicates highly efficient burst-like star formation with substantial metal-enriched outflows. NGC 5717 may therefore represent a rare example of disk growth in a massive central galaxy, enabled by external cold-gas accretion during an earlier phase of the group assembly history.

We investigate the thermal phase structures and kinematics of atomic hydrogen (H I) gas in the Draco nebula to clarify how infalling H I evolves toward cold atomic and molecular clouds. Using the DHIGLS H I 21 cm data, we apply the Regularized Optimization for Hyper-Spectral Analysis (ROHSA) algorithm to decompose the emission into spatially coherent Gaussian components, and classify them as warm neutral medium (WNM), lukewarm neutral medium (LNM), and cold neutral medium (CNM). We identify two kinematically distinct WNM components: WNM1 near $-3$ km s$^{-1}$ and WNM2 near $-30$ km s$^{-1}$. The LNM forms a broad velocity bridge between two WNMs, whereas the CNM is concentrated within a narrower intermediate velocity range. CO emission is also confined to this interval and is associated with CNM-rich regions. The H I mass fractions are $\sim 41\%$, $\sim 23\%$, $\sim 17\%$, and $\sim 19\%$ for WNM1, WNM2, LNM, and CNM, respectively, with the LNM and CNM together accounting for $\sim 36\%$ of the total H I mass. No comparably independent WNM component is recovered at intermediate velocities, although a weak broad component may remain unresolved. The observed phase structures are consistent with a dynamically compressed multiphase interaction layer in which compression increases the gas density, shortens the radiative cooling time, and drives the evolution from WNM through thermally unstable gas toward the CNM. The present interaction may both create new CNM, and further compress or grow pre-existing dense structures. Our results provide the missing link between previous evidence for dynamical interaction and molecular cloud formation in Draco, and identify CNM formation as a key step in transforming gas accreting toward the Galactic disk into cold atomic and molecular clouds.
