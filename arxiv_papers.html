The Impact of Flyby Interactions on the Rotation of Open Clusters
Authors
Kyle R. Tregoning, Jeff J. Andrews
Comments
17 pages, 6 figures, submitted for publication in ApJ, comments welcome
Primary
astro-ph.GA
Categories
astro-ph.GA astro-ph.SR
The Gaia astrometric mission has provided the first evidence for the bulk rotation of open clusters. While this rotation may arise from residual spin inherited from a cluster's parent molecular cloud, it is unclear whether this explanation always suffices, as angular momentum is carried away by escaping stars on the timescale of two-body relaxation. In particular, the recent observance of significant rotation ($v_{\rm rot} = 0.81 \pm 0.06$ km s$^{-1}$) in the dynamically old ($\approx 4$ Gyr) open cluster M67 motivates a study of another potential source of spin for Solar neighborhood clusters: tidal torquing from gravitational encounters with passing giant molecular clouds (GMCs). We derive an analytic estimate for the change in angular momentum of a cluster due to an impulsive interaction with a passing GMC, and verify this expectation by performing $N$-body simulations over a range of encounter parameters using REBOUND. For highly energetic encounters, significant angular momentum can be transferred, but the stars that receive the most angular momentum are ejected, leaving the cluster remnant with negligible net rotation. Even when considering a random walk of many GMC-cluster encounters, the integrated effects of these interactions cannot explain observations of dynamically old rotating open clusters. However, we find that within $\sim 5$ Myr post-encounter, some disrupted clusters could be confused as rotating clusters while their stars are still dispersing. Using the local density of GMCs, we estimate that up to $\approx 1\%$ of open clusters in the Milky Way may carry this faux-rotation signal.

The radioactive isotope 26Al is believed to be mainly produced by massive stars and ejected into the interstellar medium through their winds and supernovae. Due to its lifetime of 1.04 Myr, it traces recent nucleosynthesis and star formation. 60Fe is a similarly long-lived (lifetime 3.8 Myr) isotope, and only originating in ccSNe. Stellar evolution models in combination with Galactic Chemical Evolution (GCE) models tend to over-predict the 60Fe/26Al ratio. Population synthesis models that attempt to explain both, the 26Al mass in the Galaxy and its spatial distribution, only match the observational constraints with an enhanced star formation rate (SFR) of >5 Msun/yr. Classical novae may contribute to the 26Al production, potentially lowering the SFR inferred from massive stars and alleviating tension in the 60Fe/26Al ratio. However, previous studies suggest their contribution is negligible or < 10%. In this work we aim to determine the mass distribution of 26Al as a function of Galactocentric radius and estimate the contribution of (very) massive stars and classical novae to the 1.8 MeV signal. We are using 20 yr of INTEGRAL/SPI observations of the gamma-ray line at 1.8 MeV to test different assumptions on the radial structure of 26Al and compare the resulting mass distribution to different GCE models. For our baseline analysis, all tested source models prefer substantial nova contributions, with best-fit fractions of 77-84%. This alleviates tension in the 60Fe/26Al ratio and lowers the SFR from 26Al measurements accordingly to 1-2 Msun/yr. While it is difficult to gauge both contributions directly, next generation MeV telescopes can potentially identify individual massive stars, such as Gamma-2-Velorum.

The study of galaxies outside the Milky Way using observations of resolved stellar populations has experienced extraordinary growth over the past two decades. There now exist panchromatic photometry and precise astrometry for hundreds of millions of individually resolved stars in hundreds of galaxies that span $\sim10^9$ in luminosity, $\sim10^3$ in metallicity, and sample a wide range of environments. Here, we review advances in extragalactic astronomy enabled by resolved stellar population imaging. We summarize major observational programs and focus on four interconnected science areas: the star formation histories of galaxies, 'bursty' star formation, the stellar initial mass function, and the orbital histories of Local Group galaxies. We review the literature in each area, synthesize key findings, identify emerging trends, and discuss how new and future facilities (e.g., JWST, LSST, Euclid, Roman, CSST, ELTs, HWO) will enable further progress. Finally, we highlight shortcomings in current datasets and discuss promising directions for new areas of investigation.
Binary Progenitors of Electron-capture Supernovae
Authors
Liji Zhong, Yong Shao
Comments
12 pages, 8 figures, accepted for publication in MNRAS
Primary
astro-ph.HE
Categories
astro-ph.HE astro-ph.SR
Electron-capture supernovae (ECSNe) arise from a narrow progenitor parameter space between oxygen-neon white dwarf formation and iron-core collapse supernovae, governed by the competition between core growth and envelope removal. Binary interactions modify this balance through mass transfer and mergers. Using the binary population synthesis framework POSYDON, we model ECSN progenitors across metallicities from $Z/Z_\odot=2$ to $10^{-4}$, focusing on the initial parameter space, pre-supernova stripping, envelope-based classification, surviving companions, post-supernova orbits, and cosmic event rates. The lower edge of the binary ECSN initial primary mass lies near $7\,M_\odot$, owing to efficient convective overshooting in the POSYDON grids, whereas the upper edge increases with metallicity because stronger line-driven winds require larger initial masses to build carbon-oxygen cores within the ECSN range. Stable mass transfer and mergers occupy distinct regions in initial mass ratio and orbital period space. ECSNe in bound binaries are divided into hydrogen-rich Type II ($\sim30\%$), IIb ($\sim10\%$), and Ib ($\sim60\%$) outcomes according to residual hydrogen and helium envelopes. Across all channels, approximately one-third of ECSNe have a bound companion at explosion; the remaining two-thirds do not. Among these bound systems, approximately $90\%$ of the companions are main-sequence stars at explosion. These companions exhibit a bimodal spin distribution, including a rapidly rotating component associated with prior mass transfer. Evolved-star and compact-object companions each account for only a few percent. Combining the formation efficiencies with cosmic star formation and metallicity evolution models yields an intrinsic ECSN rate peaking at $z\sim2-3$ and declining at higher redshifts. Over cosmic history, ECSNe contribute approximately $1\%-4\%$ of all core-collapse supernovae.

The Central Molecular Zone (CMZ) at the center of the Galaxy provides a nearby laboratory for studying star formation under extreme physical and chemical conditions. Recent ALMA (Atacama Large Millimeter/Submillimeter Array) observations have identified 43 candidate protostellar outflows in three CMZ clouds. One of these, the Sgr C-F#2 outflow in Sagittarius C (Sgr C), has a highly collimated lobe extending approximately 0.8 pc in projection. Here we present a joint analysis of ALMA observations of SiO J=8-7, 5-4, and 2-1. We use local thermodynamic equilibrium (LTE) and non-LTE methods to constrain the SiO column densities, H2 number density, and gas temperature. The low SiO rotational temperatures and higher kinetic temperatures inferred from non-LTE methods suggest subthermal excitation. The elongated SiO morphology, shock-excited gas, position-velocity structure, and the alignment of the red shifted molecular lobe with JWST infrared knots altogether favor jet-dominated entrainment, whereas a broader disk-wind component close to the source cannot be excluded. The outflow shows asymmetries in its morphology and physical properties, which can be attributed to dense gas on the path of its lobe impeding its propagation and feedback from a nearby HII region imposing different pressure on its two sides.

Only five out of the $\sim$350 molecules detected in the interstellar medium (ISM) carry the isocyanate functional group (NCO), the essential substructure of peptide bonds in proteins. Among them are isocyanic acid (HNCO) and its most stable N-protonated isomer H$_2$NCO$^+$. Previous studies have predicted that the O-protonated isomer HNCOH$^+$ should be present in the ISM; yet this species has remained elusive. We search for the metastable HNCOH$^+$ towards the Galactic Center molecular cloud G+0.693-0.027, where H$_2$NCO$^+$ has already been detected. We use an ultra-deep broadband spectral survey carried out towards G+0.693-0.027 with the Yebes 40-m, IRAM 30-m and APEX telescopes covering nearly 150 GHz of bandwidth between 31 and 275 GHz. We report the first detection of HNCOH$^+$ in the ISM with a column density of $N$ = (9.9 $\pm$ 0.5)$\times$10$^{12}$ cm$^{-2}$, which corresponds to a molecular abundance relative to H$_2$ of (7.3$^{+1.3} _{-1.0}$)$\times$10$^{-11}$. With the measured H$_2$NCO$^+$ column density of $N$ = (2.6 $\pm$ 0.3) $\times 10^{12}$ cm$^{-2}$, this yields a HNCOH$^+$/H$_2$NCO$^+$ ratio of 3.8$^{+0.5} _{-0.4}$. This result is consistent with recent kinetic calculations that favour the formation of HNCOH$^+$ over H$_2$NCO$^+$. The detection of HNCOH$^+$ in the ISM broadens the inventory of molecules known to contain the isocyanate functional group and reinforces recent works showing that isomer abundances in the ISM do not necessarily correlate with thermodynamic stability.

Blue cores in brightest cluster galaxies (BCGs) trace recent central activity associated with cooling cluster atmospheres. We search for blue cores at $z<0.6$ in five eROSITA all-sky surveys using X-ray data, optical imaging, and optical spectroscopy. We identify 379 blue-core BCGs among 11352 systems (3.3%), although contamination increases toward higher redshift. Of these, 98 (0.86%) are X-ray bright. Their defining signature is a central $g-r$ color at least 0.2 mag bluer than the reference population within $a=20$ kpc before returning to the normal BCG envelope. Ha emission equivalent width correlates strongly with the blue continuum excess, consistent with young stellar components comprising ~0.1-1% of pre-existing stellar mass. Line ratios indicate widespread LINER-like excitation, with a larger star-forming contribution in blue Ha emitters but substantial non-stellar excitation. This activity is closely linked to the thermodynamics of the host-cluster gas. At $0.1R_{500}$, cooling times decrease from BCGs without significant Ha emission to red and then blue Ha emitters. Blue BCGs preferentially occur in clusters with $L_{\rm X,300}>10^{44}$ erg s$^{-1}$; red Ha emitters also favor higher X-ray luminosities. The blue-core fraction rises with optical richness. For systems with $t_{\rm cool}(0.1R_{500})<20$ Gyr, doubling richness increases the odds of hosting a blue-core BCG by $2.45\pm0.39$. This indicates that cluster scale carries information beyond cooling time, plausibly through the amount of gas available for cooling and condensation. The scarcity of blue BCGs without significant Ha emission disfavors isolated short bursts followed by long Ha-quiet fading phases. It can instead be explained by sustained or closely recurrent star formation over timescales much longer than 100 Myr, or by Ha persisting through additional excitation mechanisms as the young stellar component fades.

EP241021a is a peculiar fast X-ray transient exhibiting a long X-ray plateau and an optical rebrightening, both challenging the traditional jet afterglow model. Whether its nature is a stellar explosion or a tidal disruption event (TDE) remains debated. Here we present late-time HST/WFC3 and Gemini-N/GMOS observations of its host galaxy at $z=0.748$. After $\sim300$ days post-trigger, the transient emission had faded below detection, revealing a dwarf disk galaxy with an effective radius $R_e=1.7$ kpc, a stellar mass $M_\star\sim3\times10^8M_\odot$, and a high star formation rate of $\sim5\ M_\odot\ {\rm yr}^{-1}$. Combined with previous very long baseline interferometry measurements, the transient can be located in the galactic disk with a projected physical offset of $0.95\pm0.13$ kpc from the galaxy center. Host-subtracted optical light curves show that the rebrightening ceased at $\sim47$ days, followed by a sudden drop with a power-law index steeper than $-3.3$, coincident with a similar drop in the X-ray light curve. This synchronous drop in both bands is consistent with jet shut-off, and the required month-long jet activity suggests a persistently accreting compact object fed by a TDE. Among the TDE scenarios, the off-nuclear location and the starburst host galaxy slightly favor the post-supernova micro-TDE scenario in a binary system, in which a natal kick drives the newborn compact object into a tidal disruption of its companion. Nevertheless, an off-nuclear intermediate-mass black hole TDE or a stellar-mass black hole TDE induced by dynamical scattering in a dense stellar cluster cannot yet be excluded.
UnEZ: A Fully Unsupervised Self-Templating Neural Network for Redshift and Line Strength Estimation

Asha Abd, Karl Glazebrook, Colin Jacobs

As spectroscopic surveys grow, estimating galaxy properties such as metallicity, star formation rate, and redshift becomes computationally cumbersome. Methods of estimating such properties rely on template spectra or curated redshift catalogs, which fail to represent the diversity of objects seen in large-scale surveys. We present UnEZ, a fully unsupervised, physics-informed machine learning model that can predict spectroscopic redshifts and line strengths with no prior redshift or line strength labels. An encoder predicts emission and absorption line amplitudes/widths, and a fixed deterministic decoder constructs a unique template, estimated redshift, redshift confidence curve, and spectral model. UnEZ was tested on the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) Data Release 18 due to its large dataset and thoroughly vetted redshift catalog which serves as an independent benchmark. Despite the lack of templates, line strength labels, or redshift labels, UnEZ's performance competes with modern redshift estimation algorithms. At SNR > 10, UnEZ achieves an outlier fraction of 0.459% and a normalized median absolute deviation (NMAD) of $1.237 \times 10^{-4}$. At SNR > 3, UnEZ achieves an outlier fraction of 3.89% and an NMAD of $1.471 \times 10^{-4}$. Furthermore, we demonstrate how UnEZ's line strength and redshift predictions can be utilized for various purposes such as constructing template spectra required in traditional redshift estimation algorithms. UnEZ illustrates how a fully unsupervised neural network can allow redshift, among other galaxy properties, to emerge naturally from the spectra themselves, providing a template-free, label-free approach that scales to the demands of future surveys.

In this extension of our "Reigniting the FUSE" series, we have obtained new far-ultraviolet spectroscopy of 3 starbursting brightest cluster galaxies with the Cosmic Origins Spectrograph (COS) aboard HST. With these new data, in combination with archival COS data of 2 additional systems, we measure star formation rates (SFRs) from the young stellar continuum and cooling rates through $10^{5.5}$ K gas with the O VI $1032,1038\,\mathring{\rm A}$ doublet ($\dot{M}_{\rm O\,VI}$). Determining how the Milky Way warp evolves provides constraints on the formation and dynamical evolution of galactic disks. Using Apache Point Observatory Galactic Evolution Experiment Data Release 19 (APOGEE DR19) spectroscopy, we estimate ages for 222,355 red giants. With Gaia astrometry and geometric distances, we analyze 168,824 of these stars in five age bins spanning $0$--$10$ Gyr. The warp-related radial vertical-velocity ($V_Z$) profile of young red giants ($0$--$2$ Gyr) is broadly consistent with those of classical Cepheids and young open clusters. Over $9\leq R<12$ kpc, we find that the mean $V_Z$ values of the intermediate-age red giants ($2$--$4$ and $4$--$6$ Gyr) are $1.48\pm0.46$ and $1.49\pm0.52$ km s$^{-1}$ higher than that of the young population, respectively. By contrast, at $R\simeq12$--$13$ kpc, the $V_Z$ profiles of the $0$--$6$ Gyr red giants are consistent within their uncertainties. The intermediate-age enhancement persists across our robustness tests and may reflect smaller effective kinematic warp onset radii for the intermediate-age populations. The observed velocity differences may arise from the combined effects of earlier perturbations and the present-day warp, or from differences in vertical structure among stellar populations.

Context: Massive early-type galaxies (ETGs) are traditionally considered "red-and-dead" based on optical observations. However, ultraviolet (UV) spectroscopy can uncover a hidden population of young stars with a sub-one-per cent mass fraction. Aims: We measure the young stellar mass fraction in NGC 3115, a massive isolated lenticular galaxy, to test the ubiquity and spatial distribution of this component beyond previously studied ellipticals. Methods: Using SOAR/Goodman spectra and line-strength fitting, we modelled the star-formation history with a single bulk stellar population plus a young component (constant star-formation over the last 1 Gyr). Conclusions: Young stars in old, "red-and-dead" galaxies are more common than expected. Their presence across various ETGs with similar gradients indicates that residual star formation is a ubiquitous feature, offering key constraints on the baryon cycle in these galaxies.
Resolving the thermal component of stellar feedback with the "anchor-and-refine" scheme in galactic simulations

Chengzhe Li, Hui Li, Wei Cui, Federico Marinacci, Laura Sales, Mark Vogelsberger, Paul Torrey

(abridged) Stellar feedback regulates galactic baryon cycles, but galaxy-scale simulations often cannot resolve supernova cooling radii. Momentum-based subgrid models can consequently underresolve the hot gas around young stars. We develop an "anchor-and-refine" feedback scheme that anchors a host cell near each star particle, preventing its displacement and preserving a thermal-feedback reservoir. When the cooling radius is resolved, all supernova energy is injected into the host cell as thermal energy; otherwise, the host cell is refined and terminal momentum is deposited into surrounding gas cells. Idealized tests agree with high-resolution simulations, yielding consistent superbubble properties and preserving hot interiors without artificial cooling. In an isolated Milky Way-mass galaxy, the scheme captures finer X-ray-emitting structures and a sharply defined, X-ray-bright central mid-plane. It also produces a smoother star formation history than pure hybrid feedback. Hot gas dominates outflow mass loading, with the mass-loading factor plateauing at approximately 0.2 at heights z>1 kpc. Phase decomposition indicates that hot gas sustains a steady, energy-driven wind, whereas cooler outflows decline with height because of radiative cooling and gravitational fallback. These results highlight the role of thermal feedback in driving galactic winds. By preserving hot superbubble interiors and the partition of thermal and kinetic energy, anchor-and-refine improves the treatment of hot gas, star formation, and feedback at limited resolution, supporting future applications to galaxy-scale and cosmological zoom-in simulations.

eROSITA bubbles (eRBs) in our Galaxy are thought to be due to the energy injection by the central star-forming region or the central Super Massive Black Hole. Identifying the origin of these bubbles is crucial in constraining the origin of the Fermi Bubbles and the nature of the Galactic feedback. However, some recent opinions suggest that the eRBs may not even have been produced by any central source, but rather a result of a star-formation ring at $R\sim 5$ kpc. Our study, therefore, suggests that the eROSITA bubbles truly represent the nuclear outflow from our Galaxy.

We present NOEMA CO(1-0) observations of the inner $\sim$500 pc of M33 at a resolution of $0.91''\times0.37''$ ($3.7\times1.5$ pc), resolving the compact molecular gas structures and revealing their relation to recent massive star formation. While numerous studies have shown that satellite galaxies experience declining star formation rates after infall, direct comparisons across different surveys and redshifts are complicated by varying sample selections, environmental metrics, and techniques used to derive galaxy properties. In this work, we select satellite galaxies from the highly complete Galaxy and Mass Assembly (GAMA) Survey and the Deep Extragalactic VIsible Legacy Survey (DEVILS) to investigate environmental quenching out to z~0.4. These surveys derive galaxy properties and environments in a consistent manner, minimizing systematic biases and enabling a robust comparison across redshift. Using projected phase space as a proxy for infall time, we quantify the evolution of the quenched fraction and trace how satellites evolve relative to the star-forming main sequence (SFMS). We find consistent trends between GAMA and DEVILS, with satellites exhibiting a steady decline in star formation relative to the SFMS at a rate of ~0.14 dex/Gyr following infall. Extending this analysis to include visual morphology, we find similar declining trends for both early- and late-type systems. Overall, we find no significant redshift evolution between GAMA and DEVILS, indicating that group and cluster environments have a comparable impact on the star formation activity of satellite galaxies over the past ~4.5 Gyr.

The Milky Way is commonly depicted as having four major spiral arms, including the Norma-Outer arm, and a central stellar bar whose properties remain debated. Masers associated with young high-mass stars have been used to trace a segment of the "Norma arm" extending from one end of the Galaxy's central stellar bar. Recent work suggests that these masers may reside along a spiral arm or occupy the dominant x1 stellar orbits that give the bar its shape. We now use a new generation of N-body, hydrodynamic simulations to argue that these million-year old masers instead trace the Galactic bar's Quadrant I shock front or, equivalently, its dust lane. This implies that the bar dust-lane is star-forming, shedding new light on our understanding of star formation processes in the Milky Way. By associating the masers with the simulated shock front, we determine the bar's parameters to be: a ~ 4.3 +/- 0.2 kpc, b ~ 2.3 +/- 0.2 kpc, phi = 20 (+5/-2) deg, Omega_p <~ 40 km s^-1 kpc^-1, representing the bar's semi-major and semi-minor half-lengths, inclination angle and pattern speed, respectively. This raises questions about the Milky Way's spiral structure and suggests that the Galactic bar is long, slow and star-forming.

Open clusters exhibit diverse spatial structures that provide insight into their formation and dynamical evolution. We investigate the relationship between stellar mass segregation, network topology, and dynamical age in 871 dynamically unevolved open clusters ($τ= t/t_{\rm rh} \leq 1$) from Gaia-based catalogs. We construct three-dimensional k-nearest-neighbor graphs and measure Degree Centrality (DC), Closeness Centrality (CL), and Betweenness Centrality (BC). For each measure, we define a network-based mass segregation ratio (MSR) by comparing the mean centrality of the most massive stars with that of all cluster members. We test the stability of these measures across connectivity parameters $k=5$--20 and compare them with the classical 3D MSR, $Λ_{\rm MSR,3D}$, and dynamical age. All three network ratios show positive correlations with $Λ_{\rm MSR,3D}$, strongest for closeness centrality, while their correlations with dynamical age are weak. These results demonstrate that network analysis provides a complementary approach to studying stellar structure and mass segregation in open clusters.

We detect two high-confidence star-forming sources in the environment of a chemically near-pristine ([Fe/H] $<-3.66~;2σ)$ Damped Ly$α$ system (DLA) using Keck/KCWI. These are found via the hydrogen Ly$α$ emission line at $Δv=-1200$ kms$^{-1}$ (LAE1) and $Δv = -200$ kms$^{-1}$ (LAE2) relative to the DLA redshift ($z_{\rm abs} = 3.078$) at an impact parameter of $b=66$ physical kpc and $b=137$ physical kpc. We measure Ly$α$ fluxes of $F(\rm Lyα)_{\rm LAE1} =4.4\pm 0.7\times10^{-18}~\rm erg\,s^{-1}\,cm^{-2}$ and $F(\rm Lyα)_{\rm LAE2} =3.8\pm 0.9\times10^{-18}~\rm erg\,s^{-1}\,cm^{-2}$. This marks the first detection of star formation in the environment of a chemically near-pristine DLA. We use red channel Keck/KCRM data to place limits on the associated He II 1640 A emission flux of these sources, $F(\rm He\,II)<3.8\times10^{-19}~\rm erg\,s^{-1}\,cm^{-2}~(3σ)$ and $F(\rm He\,II) <3.7\times10^{-19}~\rm erg\,s^{-1}\,cm^{-2}~(3σ)$. The weak and narrow properties of the Ly$α$ emission, combined with the inferred position on the mass-metallicity relation, suggest a metal-paucity of these galaxies. Future rest-frame optical observations are required to pin down the metallicity of LAE1 and LAE2 and test if chemically near-pristine DLAs can be used as a signpost to search for primitive, metal-poor galaxies 2 Gyrs after the Big Bang.

We present a detailed study of the open cluster Berkeley 31 using Gaia DR3 astrometry, Gaia-ESO Survey DR5.1 spectroscopy, and multi-wavelength photometry. We identify 329 probable cluster members from their proper motions and trigonometric parallaxes and use them to examine the cluster's fundamental parameters, stellar populations, and Galactic orbit. The cluster is located at a distance of $6024 \pm 181$ pc, with a color excess of $E(B-V)=0.148 \pm 0.047$ mag, and has a mean metallicity of [Fe/H] $=-0.36 \pm 0.04$ dex and $\langle [α/{\rm Fe}] \rangle=0.21 \pm 0.04$ dex. Isochrone fitting gives an age of $3.7 \pm 0.2$ Gyr, consistent with the independent chemical-clock estimate of $3.72 \pm 1.42$ Gyr. The systemic radial velocity is $57.89 \pm 0.08$ km s$^{-1}$. We find that about 38% of the MS stars are unresolved binary candidates with $q \gtrsim 0.5$. We identify 62 BSSs and 2 YSS candidates, including 48 BSSs and both YSSs not previously reported in the literature. We constructed multi-wavelength SEDs for these stars, including Swift/UVOT data for 28 BSSs. Two BSSs show clear ultraviolet excesses and are consistent with compact hot companions, one with a hot WD and the other with a pre-ELM WD. The present orbital parameters give a guiding radius of $12.29 \pm 0.29$ kpc and an eccentricity of $0.11 \pm 0.01$. Comparison with the estimated chemical birth radius indicates a moderate outward shift of the guiding center, with the present orbital position also contributing to the radial displacement. These results consistently describe Berkeley 31 as an intermediate-age, metal-poor outer-disk cluster and place its stellar populations in the context of its Galactic dynamical history.

We present the deepest HI observations of the two systems with Dproj < 100 kpc, and $Δ$ vsys < 200 km/s in the MHONGOOSE survey. The MeerKAT observations have a 3$σ$ NHI = 10$^{17.9}$ cm$^{-2}$ over 16 km/s at 4 kpc scales. The new data detect HI in seven galaxies -- the interacting NGC 3511/3513 pair, and an interacting triplet (NGC 4781, NGC 4790 and UGCA 308) along with two new (2MASX J1255 and MKT 25413) dwarf satellites. Six galaxies lie on the star-forming main sequence, and their HI gas fractions are typical for such systems. In contrast, MKT 25413 has a log(MHI/Msun) = 5.7, which is the lowest MHI detected of a galaxy that is not a Milky Way satellite. This low surface brightness dwarf galaxy shows an anomalously low star formation and HI fraction for its stellar mass. We find ubiquitous low column density HI surrounding the galaxies, which is likely a consequence of mild tidal interactions from fly-bys in the past few hundred megayears. We derive the stellar and H$α$ kinematics from VLT/MUSE observations and the H2 kinematics from ALMA observations. The kinematics of the stellar and multiphase interstellar medium of the interacting galaxies display ordered rotation, and the atomic-to-molecular gas ratios are indistinguishable from star-forming field galaxies. The stellar morphology of all the interacting galaxies (except the low-mass dwarf UGCA 308) does not show major disruptions, and there is no stellar component associated with the HI tails, bridges or clouds in the intra-group mediums, down to $μ_{g}$ = 29 mag arcsec$^{-2}$. We surmise that the mild environmental interactions in the close systems are able to pull the diffuse HI out of the disk and redistribute it. The gas is not in equilibrium, although it remains bound and is in the process of falling back onto the disk.

We present the Star-formation from Photometry through the Addition of Medium-bands (SPAM) survey, a 62.9 hr JWST/NIRCam imaging program to add ten new filters, nine medium-band filters, and one broad-band filter to the CEERS legacy dataset. We describe the observation strategy, present the data, and detail the data-reduction process. We include a complete reduction of imaging in the legacy CEERS footprint, inclusive of all publicly available photometric parallel and GO observations within the legacy CEERS footprint. We present a multi-band photometric and photometric redshift catalog which includes all medium- and broad-band filters available on NIRCam. We find that adding medium bands from the SPAM and MINERVA programs improves photometric redshifts. Leo T, a Milky Way (MW) satellite, is the lowest-mass gas-rich galaxy known to show recent star formation, making it an excellent laboratory for investigating star formation in the ultraviolet (UV) regime within a low-density, metal-poor environment. We present a far-UV (FUV) study using Ultraviolet Imaging Telescope (UVIT) data, aiming to characterize FUV star-forming clusters (SFCs), identify FUV-emitting stars, constrain their properties, and explore star formation in an extremely low-mass, metal-poor galaxy. We examined the galaxy's FUV extent and compared it with the H I distribution. We extracted thirty SFCs and derived their properties, including area, luminosity, and star formation rate (SFR), and compared them with previous studies. FUV-emitting stars and their stellar parameters were identified through cross-matching with catalogs from the Hubble Space Telescope and the Multi Unit Spectroscopic Explorer (MUSE). We also estimated the global SFR and star formation efficiencies (SFEs) associated with previously identified CO clouds. The FUV luminosities of the SFCs confirm the absence of massive O and B-type stars in Leo T. The FUV-emitting stellar population consists of main-sequence (MS) stars, blue loop (BL) stars, horizontal branch stars, and emission-line (EM) stars. The upper MS and EM stars are concentrated near the optical center, whereas the lower MS and BL stars are more spatially extended. We find that five out of the six FUV-emitting stars with MUSE spectroscopy are extremely metal-poor ([Fe/H] $\leq$ -3). The (NUV - r) color profile indicates that the UV emission is dominated by stellar populations younger than 1 Gyr out to a radius $\lt$ 185 pc. Despite the recent detection of CO(1-0) emission, no SFCs are associated with the molecular clouds, which exhibit extremely low SFEs. The spatial distribution of the SFCs suggests a directional interaction with the MW.
