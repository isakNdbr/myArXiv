We observed 69 molecular cloud regions at 450 and 850 $μ$m with the POL-2 polarimeter on the James Clerk Maxwell Telescope and matched the maps to a common $12.6''$ beam. Polarization fractions are typically lower at 450 $μ$m. The median of 30 measured ratios is $P_{450}/P_{850} = 0.69 \pm 0.02_{\rm sys} \pm 0.03_{\rm stat}$. Across 35 regions with reliable spectral indices of total ($α$) and polarized ($γ$) intensity, the medians over sources are $\langleα\rangle = 3.33$ and $\langleγ\rangle = 2.85$-$3.04$. The median $γ- α$ is $-0.57$ to $-0.45$, with $γ< α$ in 24-27 regions. These ranges span the two extreme placements of $3σ$ limits for polarized intensity below the noise floor. The position angles at the two wavelengths agree within $10^\circ$ in 10 of 13 regional medians, disfavoring a geometric origin that would rotate the angles. The medians of $γ-α$ in 9 low-mass, 19 high-mass and 7 Central Molecular Zone (CMZ) regions are not ordered low-mass $<$ high-mass $<$ CMZ (Jonckheere-Terpstra $p = 0.43$-$0.70$). A difference among classes is not robustly detected. The Kruskal-Wallis $p = 0.056$-$0.078$ becomes $p = 0.026$-$0.041$ when the Rosette Molecular Cloud alone is excluded, although the high-mass class has the lowest median in every treatment. The classes follow a distance sequence (the beam spans 0.008-0.51 pc), coupling environment and physical scale. Within clouds, the 450 $μ$m polarization fraction decreases with Herschel column density in all 7 fields with sufficient dynamic range. Coefficients from pooled pixels mix trends within clouds, differences between clouds, and the influence of OMC 1. They therefore do not define a single law for individual pixels. The wavelength dependence across sources provides a benchmark for grain alignment. We cannot order it by environment and do not propose a unique mechanism.

Extended main-sequence turn-offs (eMSTOs) observed in open clusters younger than $2~\mathrm{Gyr}$ are commonly attributed to a spread in stellar rotation rates. Tidal synchronization in close binary systems has been proposed as a possible mechanism for producing slowly rotating stars. To test this hypothesis, we measured the radial-velocity (RV) dispersions of slow-rotator samples associated with the eMSTOs of eight open clusters using ESO VLT/GIRAFFE and SALT/RSS spectroscopy. We extended the analysis to 40 open clusters using Gaia DR3 RV measurements. The observed cluster RV dispersions are generally substantially smaller than those predicted for binary populations dominated by close systems with orbital periods for which tidal synchronization is expected to be effective. In particular, all 40 clusters in the Gaia sample have RV dispersions below the prediction of the $P=10$ d binary-population model. These results suggest that close, tidally synchronized binaries are unlikely to be the dominant origin of slow-rotator populations in young and intermediate-age open clusters, although a contribution from undetected or wider binary systems cannot be excluded.

Measuring lithium abundance in galaxies beyond the Milky Way (MW) remains a challenging task. In these systems, only stars close to the tip of the red giant branch (RGB) are sufficiently bright to be observed spectroscopically.Their A(Li) are expected to be altered by mixing episodes that occur during the stellar evolution, making the interpretation of measured abundances more complex. In this context, we present A(Li) of 209 RGB stars in the Small Magellanic Cloud (SMC),observed with FLAMES@VLT. Approximately one-third of the sample shows a Li line detected at a significance level above 5sigma, while about half of the sample has detections above 3sigma; for the remaining stars, only upper limits can be derived. This sample exhibits a significant star-to-star scatter in A(Li). Using theoretical models, we find that the observed scatter can be likely explained by the combination of 2 effects.First,our stars span a wide range of ages, masses, [Fe/H]. Thermohaline mixing, one of the leading mechanisms proposed to explain the extra mixing occurring at the RGB bump, predicts different levels of A(Li) depletion depending on stellar mass and [Fe/H], naturally contributing to the observed scatter in A(Li). Second, in the SMC, the enrichment in A(Li) driven by novae is expected to occur at lower [Fe/H] than in the MW, because the SMC has experienced a lower star formation efficiency than the MW. This is also supported by the rise of A(Li) with decreasing age, as measured in 3 SMC globular clusters. Furthermore, we report the first identification of 4 low-mass Li-rich giant stars in the SMC, reinforcing the existence of Li-rich stars in extra-galactic systems. All these results demonstrate that A(Li) measured in stars close to the RGB tip, still provide valuable constraints on both stellar/galactic chemical evolution of A(Li) in external galaxies.

Several metal-poor Galactic globular clusters exhibit substantial star-to-star $r$-process abundance variations at nearly fixed [Fe/H], with the ensemble suggesting smaller variations toward increasing metallicity. We investigate whether this chemical structure can be inherited from incomplete mixing in the galactic environments in which the clusters formed. Using parsec-scale simulations of rare-element transport in a supernova-driven turbulent disk, we follow the redistribution of $r$-process ejecta and characterize the abundance structure of gas selected to trace star-forming environments. Rare enrichment events generate strong [Eu/Fe] inhomogeneities that persist across a range of physical scales. As chemical evolution proceeds, the available abundance variance decreases, while spatial averaging over larger regions reduces the differences between their characteristic abundances. The simulations provide substantial pre-collapse $r$-process variance at low [Fe/H] and show the same broad decline toward higher metallicity suggested by the globular-cluster measurements. Our results support an interpretation in which at least some of the observed $r$-process variations are inherited from pre-existing chemical structure in the natal environment, allowing globular clusters to preserve a fossil record of the mixing history between rare enrichment and star formation.

We present the first dedicated $v\sin i$ survey of blue straggler stars (BSS) in the old open cluster Collinder 261 ($t=6.7$ Gyr), based on VLT/FLAMES-GIRAFFE spectroscopy of 40 BSS and 42 turn-off/subgiant (TO/SGB) reference stars. Seventeen of the 39 BSS entering the statistics (44%) rotate faster than 40 km/s, whereas all TO/SGB reference stars are slow rotators; fast rotation is strongly linked to spectroscopic binarity. TESS light curves identify seven eclipsing or ellipsoidal binaries with $P_{orb}=0.5$-2.1 d, all rotating at the velocity set by tidal synchronisation, so in these systems the fast rotation is maintained by a close post-mass-transfer orbit and does not date the formation event. Differential C, O, Ba, and Fe abundances for the 22 slow rotators reveal that 62% carry the surface-pollution signature of mass transfer, in two distinct patterns separated by an empty ~1 dex gap in [Ba/Fe]: a Ba-rich group tracing AGB donors and a C/O-enhanced, Ba-normal group tracing donors below the $s$-process activation mass. The BSS iron abundance is consistent with the cluster value; the few apparent Fe excesses are partly explained by differential reddening and do not affect the abundance pattern. The correlated C-O enrichment is opposite to the depletion seen in globular-cluster BSS, indicating binaries wide enough for donors to reach the AGB. With $A^+_{r_h}=0.171\pm0.034$ and $f_{FR-BSS}=44\pm8$%, Collinder 261 extends the globular-cluster rotation-density trends into the open-cluster regime: its BSS were assembled by a continuously operating binary channel, and their chemistry records the dynamical environment of the host cluster.

Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) emission is a powerful tool for the study of the interstellar medium (ISM) in both Galactic and extragalactic contexts. It can reveal ionization state, radiation field, metallicity, composition, and ISM structure as sculpted by processes like turbulence and stellar feedback. Here we use NASA's SPHEREx to produce clean, high-resolution PAH maps at spectral resolution R $\sim$ 35 (compared to WISE at R $\sim$ 2). Prior treatments of the SPHEREx linear variable filter data seek to convert them to more conventional band-averaged maps that can leave striping artifacts associated with spatially variable wavelength sampling, especially at emission lines. In contrast, we deliver a scalable forward-modeling method capable of generating high-quality all-sky SPHEREx emission-line maps at 8.5 arcsec FWHM angular resolution (on 6.4 arcsec pixels), while naturally producing maps without visible striping. We reformat the data into a pixel-level database, mask stellar contamination, and infill missing pixels with a conditional, data-driven, anisotropic Gaussian process. We apply our method to produce 3.3 $μ$m and 3.4 $μ$m PAH and Paschen-$α$ emission maps of a set of fields spanning a range of Galactic and extragalactic conditions including the Rosette nebula, the Corona Australis molecular cloud, M33, and M31. We demonstrate the quality and synergies of our SPHEREx 3.3 $μ$m PAH maps by comparisons with other available gas and dust tracer maps and achieve sensitivities up to $σ(I_{3.3}) = 2.7 \times 10^{-8}$ erg s$^{-1}$ cm$^{-2}$ sr$^{-1}$. We estimate that a two-year extension of the SPHEREx survey, with modest modifications to the scanning strategy, would match or exceed the PAH sensitivity of WISE over the whole sky.

Molecular outflows probe protostellar accretion and mass transfer from clump scales to forming stars. We present an ALMA study of CO (J=2-1) outflows toward 13 massive clumps from the SQUALO sample, spanning a broad evolutionary range and exhibiting clump-scale infall. We identify 51 high-velocity CO lobes associated with 31 outflow systems and derive their physical and dynamical properties assuming local thermodynamic equilibrium. Outflow inferred accretion rates are typically 10^{-5}-10^{-3} Msun/yr, consistent with active massive star formation. The maximum outflow velocity shows little dependence on candidate driving-core mass, indicating that it is not a reliable tracer of protostellar mass in clustered environments. Outflow momentum flux correlates positively with clump surface density, whereas accretion rate shows a tentative, statistically nonsignificant decrease with increasing virial parameter. Total outflow inferred accretion rate shows a tentative positive relation with clump-scale infall rate but remains systematically lower, suggesting that the detected outflows represent only part of the large-scale mass flux. The clump L/M ratio moderately anticorrelates with projected outflow length, although projection, observational limitations, and the embedded source population prevent a unique evolutionary interpretation. Together with the continuum derived fragmentation properties, our results show that compact cores embedded within infalling clumps drive the outflows. The tentative relation between clump-scale infall and protostellar accretion suggests a possible connection across these scales, although our observations do not directly trace mass transfer from filaments to individual protostars.

Stars form in clustered environments, and then the stars may congregate into star clusters. The timescale on which individual stars form is not necessarily the same as the timescale on which star clusters assemble. Therefore, developing methods to probe these different timescales is a crucial part of understanding star cluster assembly and early evolution. Dynamical ages can be constructed from the kinematics of individual stars in clusters in the solar neighborhood using Gaia data. Using a star formation simulation, we test five different dynamical age methods. We divide dynamical ages into two approaches: methods which trace the size evolution of the cluster, and methods which trace the trajectories of individual stars. We confirm that size methods cannot accurately derive ages in bound clusters whereas methods tracing individual stars can be applied to both bound and unbound systems. We find that different dynamical age methods trace different epochs of star cluster formation and assembly: The method using stars with the oldest traceback ages provides an upper limit on stellar ages and the method using the median traceback ages is the best proxy for isochronal ages. Methods which trace the size evolution of the cluster best trace the onset of cluster expansion. Our work suggests that the median traceback time of stars moving away from the cluster provides the most reliable proxy for isochronal age, and that this method can be applied to any expanding system.

We study the resolved stellar kinematics and star formation histories, and their dependence on the environment, in a sample of 218 intermediate redshift (0.1 < z < 1) galaxies located in the CANDELS and COSMOS fields, observed with VLT/MUSE. The sample covers galaxies with stellar masses ranging M_star ~10^8 - 10^12 M_sun, including both star-forming and quiescent systems. For 113 galaxies, found in dense environments of galaxy groups, we derive the spin parameter ($λ_{R}$), while for the rest of the sample, we use data from previous papers in this series. Given that the surveys used in this work are flux-limited, the $λ_{R}$ distribution of our full sample lacks the bimodality observed in the local Universe, but the subsample of galaxies at z < 0.6 resembles the galaxy population in the nearby Universe, in terms of mass, sizes, and stellar population properties, more than galaxies at z > 0.6. We classified the galaxies into fast and slow rotators, finding that ~8% (17/218) of the galaxies are slow-rotating. The fraction of slow rotators does not change significantly down to z = 0, but we find that in the z ~ 0 sample, the spin parameter is, on average, higher than at higher redshifts. In our subsample at z > 0.6, we observed evidence for the existence of the kinematic morphology-density relation. The slow rotators in our sample mostly assemble their stellar mass in one or two main episodes of formation, and they finished assembling their mass via star formation by redshift of z $\sim$ 1. It is however, not clear if the loss of angular momentum can be associated with the last star formation episode. Based on their location in the mass - size diagram, the intermediate-redshift galaxies are consistent with being the progenitors of the most massive slow rotators observed at z~0, where the subsequent growth is facilitated through gas-poor (dry) mergers.

The Tully--Fisher relation (TFR) and the specific angular momentum--mass relation (SAMR) link the baryonic content of a disc to its kinematics and dynamics. Hence, these scaling relations should bear the imprints of any process removing baryons or altering their kinematics, such as those acting in clusters of galaxies. We test this using the MUSE survey "GAs Stripping Phenomena in galaxies" (GASP), which targets field, group, and cluster members at $z \approx 0.05$ and various stages of ram pressure stripping. For the 81 discs with favourable inclinations, we measure the distribution and kinematics of stars and ionised gas, and their specific angular momenta. Within their scatter, the stellar and baryonic relations show no significant dependence on the large scale environment: the TFRs have slopes $α\approx 3.6$ and $\approx 3.5$ for any kinematic tracer, and the SAMRs have $α\approx 0.65$--$0.75$, as predicted by tidal torque theory. The same holds for the ten S0-S0/as in our sample, consistent with the "faded spiral" formation scenario. Only the gas SAMR shows an environmental signature, with ram pressure stripped galaxies lying $\approx 0.16$~dex below unperturbed ones. This offset is mostly due to their enhanced star formation rate, and thus higher gas mass when inferred through the Schmidt-Kennicutt law, rather than to kinematic perturbations or disc truncation. Our results suggest that, within the area probed by MUSE ($\lesssim6$ disc scale lengths), these discs have preserved the angular momentum acquired at formation not only from cluster processes and pre-processing in filaments, but also from mergers and ejective feedback acting in any environment.

We present SEDust, an automated pipeline for spectral energy distribution (SED) fitting and the estimation of stellar mass-loss and dust-production rates. We apply this pipeline to quantify the mass-loss and dust injection rates in the dwarf galaxy NGC~6822, focusing on long-period variables (LPVs) and pulsating asymptotic giant branch (AGB) stars as major contributors to dust feedback into the interstellar medium. The total stellar mass-loss rate is found to be $(3.70 \pm 1.16) \times 10^{-3}\ M_{\odot} \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$. Two alternative methods (an LPV-based estimate and DESK) give totals that differ from this value by factors of $\sim$2.3 (lower) and $\sim$2.4 (higher), respectively, which we adopt as an estimate of the method-dependent systematic uncertainty. A positive correlation between stellar luminosity, intrinsic reddening caused by circumstellar dust self-extinction, and mass-loss rate is observed. The specific mass-return rate from AGB stars is $(7.40 \pm 2.30) \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$, indicating that mass return from AGB stars alone is insufficient to sustain the current star formation rate of NGC~6822. This result implies the need for external gas accretion or additional mass-return mechanisms.

One of the main steps in data reduction for ground-based astronomy is sky subtraction. For extended objects, it is necessary to observe dedicated sky frames, usually with airmass values similar to the target's, using different pointing strategies. However, it is not well documented how different observing strategies can affect the science end product. In particular, with the use of the MUSE/VLT, this can yield distinct stellar population products, such as age, metallicity, and α-enhancement. To address this, we considered two case studies seeking to investigate: (i) the sky stability in observations taken consecutively over a period of 2 hours with thin sky transparency and airmass values of 1.25-1.05, and (ii) the impact of different sky conditions on the same target. Among our main results, we find that for galaxies with 0.0014 < z < 0.0077, the sky line [NI] at λ5200 coincides with the Mgb bandpass, which is essential for metallicity measurements, highlighting the need for proper sky subtraction. In addition, for regions with relative fluxes above 10 with respect to the sky in NGC1097, sky frames with variation up to ~18 % still retrieve consistent stellar population properties - in this case, this corresponds to flux values of >1500 x 10^-20 erg/s/cm^2 and Johnson-V ~19.0. In contrast, when comparing data from different nights for NGC1433, fainter regions suffer greatly from distinct sky conditions, especially in metallicity measurements, which range within +/- 0.2, strongly affecting the interpretation of stellar mass assembly and the origin of the gas (i.e., mergers might bring metal-poor gas, while an in-situ burst of star formation can yield metal-rich stars). This study is the first of its kind and highlights the importance of proper sky subtraction, providing insights regarding data limitations and observational strategies for future observations with MUSE.
